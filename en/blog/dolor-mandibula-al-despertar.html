Waking up with jaw pain

  1. Home
  2. ›
  3. Blog
  4. ›
  5. Waking up with jaw pain

By Mar Escolano Morell · 25 September 2026 · 3 min read

It is one of the most common reasons people arrive at the clinic without realising it was a physiotherapy matter at all. They wake with a tired jaw, sometimes a headache at the temples, and have spent months assuming they simply sleep badly.

What is happening while you sleep

During the day, the upper and lower teeth barely touch: a few minutes in total, swallowing and chewing. The rest of the time the jaw hangs relaxed. At night, though, some people hold their teeth clenched for hours, or slide them side to side without knowing it. This is what is known as bruxism.

The muscle that closes the jaw is, for its size, one of the strongest in the body. It is built for short, very powerful bursts, not for holding a contraction for six hours straight. When it does, you wake up the way your calf would after a night spent on tiptoe.

That is why the pain is worst on getting up and usually eases through the morning. It is not that you slept awkwardly: it is that the muscle has been working all night.

Why it hurts where you did not expect

Many people do not arrive saying their jaw hurts. They arrive with headaches at the temples, an ear that aches with nothing wrong with it, dizziness, or a neck that will not let go however much they stretch it.

The explanation is that the jaw joint and the upper cervical vertebrae share muscles and share the same nerve pathways. The brain receives the signal but does not always get the sender right. An overloaded chewing muscle can refer pain to the temple, the ear or behind the eye with a consistency that is striking once you examine it.

It is also why treating only the jaw tends to give relief that does not last. If the neck is left out of the treatment, the problem comes back.

What almost never works on its own

A night guard is the first step in many consultations, and rightly so: it protects the teeth from wear, which is irreversible damage. But it is worth understanding what it does and what it does not. A guard stops the teeth from suffering. It does not stop the clenching.

In fact there are people who clench exactly as much with the guard in, and keep waking with the same pain, because the muscle carries on working. They have protected the tooth and have not touched the cause of the symptom.

The mistake, then, is not wearing a guard. It is assuming that settles it, and never looking at the muscle, the joint or the neck.

What can be done

The first step is working out which part of the problem is muscular, which part is in the joint and which part comes from the neck. That is examined: how wide the mouth opens, whether it deviates on opening, whether there is a click, which muscles hurt when pressed and what pain they reproduce.

From there, treatment usually combines manual work on the chewing muscles — some of it from inside the mouth, with a glove, which is the only way to reach some of them — treatment of the upper neck, and exercise to restore control of the movement.

And one part that is not technical and often changes the most: recognising when you clench. Many people discover, once they start paying attention, that they do it during the day too, at the computer or driving. That is something you can act on consciously.

When it is worth having it looked at

Not every jaw ache needs treatment. Occasional soreness after a tense day, or after a long dental appointment, usually settles by itself within days.

It is worth seeking help when the pain returns in the mornings over weeks, when the mouth no longer opens as far as it did, when locking appears — that moment when the jaw gets stuck and opening or closing is hard — or when it comes with frequent headaches nobody has managed to explain.

At the clinic we frequently work alongside the dentist, because parts of the problem are theirs and parts are ours. If what we see belongs to another professional, we say so.

Frequently asked questions

Can bruxism be cured?

It is managed better than it is cured. The causes are several and not all of them are in the mouth. What can be done, and is felt fairly quickly, is reducing the pain, restoring the opening and easing the load on the muscle.

I wear a guard and it still hurts. Am I using it wrong?

Probably not. The guard protects the tooth from wear, which is its job, but it does not stop the muscle clenching. Continuing pain is consistent with that, and it is a good reason to look at the muscle and the joint.

Is it related to stress?

In many cases yes, though not in all and never on its own. There are factors in the joint itself, in the bite and in sleep that also count. Calling it only stress oversimplifies a problem that rarely has a single cause.

Does a hand have to go inside my mouth?

Part of the treatment is done from the inside, with a glove, because some muscles cannot be reached from outside. It is explained beforehand, done with your consent and can be stopped at any point. It is not painful, though it feels odd at first.
