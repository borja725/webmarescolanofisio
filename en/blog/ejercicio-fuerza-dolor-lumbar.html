Why strength training keeps your back from going again

  1. Home
  2. ›
  3. Blog
  4. ›
  5. Why strength training keeps your back from going again

By Mar Escolano Morell · 25 September 2026 · 4 min read

Anyone whose back has gone once knows the story: the trivial movement, the stab of pain, and three days walking sideways. What many people do not know is that a first episode is the best predictor of a second, and that one thing consistently reduces that risk.

What is happening when your back goes

It is almost always an episode of what we call non-specific low back pain. Non-specific does not mean nobody knows what is going on: it means there is no single broken structure that explains the pain, and that the picture comes from a combination of factors. It is by far the most common case.

The movement that sets it off is usually absurd: bending for a sock, twisting to reach something on the back seat. That is why it is so bewildering. But that movement is not the cause, it is the last straw. Behind it is an area that had been tolerating less load than it was being asked for.

The good news, worth saying early, is that the vast majority of these episodes settle within weeks. The bad news is that, without doing anything differently, they come back.

Why rest is not the answer

For decades the advice was bed rest. We now know it makes the outlook worse: people who rest too much take longer to recover and are more likely to have the pain become persistent.

The reason makes sense on a moment's thought. A back does not hurt because it is broken, but because it is sensitised and has lost tolerance. Rest reduces that tolerance further, so on returning to normal life the area is less prepared than before. Each episode leaves the next one a little closer.

Staying active within what you can tolerate, even with some pain, is now the first-line recommendation across the whole literature on low back pain.

What strength training actually does

A widespread idea needs dismantling here: that muscles hold the spine up like a mast about to topple, and that they must be strengthened so it does not move. The spine does not need holding up. It is a robust structure and it is built to move.

What strength does is something else, and it is more useful to understand: <strong>it raises the load the area tolerates before it complains</strong>. Tissue that regularly works under load adapts to it. Tissue that never sees any does not. That is why the trivial movement floors the person who does not train and not the one who does.

And there is a second effect, less talked about and sometimes more important: people who train regularly stop being afraid to move their back. Fear of movement is one of the strongest predictors that back pain will become chronic, and it is treated by moving, not by being told not to worry.

It is not just the core

The word core has done a lot of damage. It has come to mean abdominals and planks, and it has been sold as the answer to all back pain.

Studies comparing core-focused programmes with general strength programmes do not find the former superior. What comes up again and again is that <strong>what matters is training at all, not which particular exercise is chosen</strong>.

A programme covering legs, hips and trunk - squat, deadlift, pushing and pulling, in whatever variation a person can manage - tends to work better than thirty minutes of planks, because it resembles what the back will actually have to do on an ordinary Tuesday.

How to start without causing trouble

Two principles, and they avoid most of the problems.

<strong>Progress slowly.</strong> Injury rarely comes from the weight; it comes from the jump - going from nothing to a lot in a fortnight. Tissue adapts, but it needs time to do so, and that time cannot be hurried by enthusiasm.

<strong>Keep it going.</strong> This is the factor that weighs most and is met least. A programme done for eight weeks and abandoned leaves the back where it started within months. Something modest sustained for years beats a perfect plan that lasts one.

If you are starting from a recent episode or from pain that has been going on a while, an assessment is what determines where to begin and what to leave alone for now. Preventing and treating are not the same thing.

When to get it looked at

Most back pain needs neither scans nor urgent treatment. But some signs are worth having checked: pain travelling down the leg below the knee, pins and needles or loss of strength in the foot, pain that has not eased at all over several weeks, or pain that persistently wakes you at night.

It is also worth seeing someone after a third or fourth episode. That repetition is not bad luck: it is the sign that the area is asking for something it is not getting.

Frequently asked questions

Can I lift weights if my back hurts?

Generally yes, adapting what you do and how much load. What needs avoiding is the exercise that reproduces the pain as it is, not exercise itself. After a recent acute episode it is worth having someone assess where to start.

Is lifting from the floor bad for you?

No. It is one of the most natural movements there is and the back is built for it. What does cause trouble is lifting a lot without ever having trained for it. Training that movement with progressive load is in fact one of the best ways to protect it.

Do I need a back support belt?

For everyday life, no. It can make sense occasionally in jobs with very heavy loads, but wearing one constantly has the drawback that the area gets used to being held from outside. The aim is the opposite.

Does posture matter as much as people say?

Less than is believed. There is no perfect posture, and the evidence linking any particular posture to back pain is far weaker than the usual talk suggests. Moving often and tolerating load matter more than holding an ideal position.

What if I have never done any sport?

That is precisely the profile with most to gain. No gym or equipment is needed: you start with your own body weight and progress from there. The hard part is not starting, it is not stopping.
