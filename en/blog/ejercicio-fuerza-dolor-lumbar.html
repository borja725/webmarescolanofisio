Why strength training keeps your back from going again

  1. Home
  2. ›
  3. Blog
  4. ›
  5. Why strength training keeps your back from going again

By Mar Escolano Morell · 25 September 2026 · 3 min read

It is one of the things we repeat most in the treatment room, and one of the hardest to get across: strength training is not only for recovering from back pain. It is, above all, what stops it coming back.

When your back goes

The scene is always much the same. Some trivial movement - bending for something, turning to pick up a bag - and suddenly the back locks. It hurts to move, straightening up is hard, and two or three days are spent walking sideways.

What baffles people most is exactly that: the movement was nothing. Nobody understands how bending to pick up a sock can leave them like this when on Saturday they shifted a washing machine without trouble.

The explanation is that the movement is not the cause. It is the last straw. Behind it is an area that had been coping with more than it was prepared for.

The problem is not the episode: it is that it returns

Most of these episodes settle within weeks, and that is why they get shrugged off. The problem is what comes afterwards.

Anyone whose back has gone once is considerably more likely to have it go again. And from the third or fourth time something else starts to set in: the sense of having a fragile back, the fear of certain movements, and the habit of giving things up just in case.

That is the part that genuinely changes someone's life, and it is the part that can be avoided.

Why strength training prevents it

Tissue that regularly works under load adapts to that load. Tissue that never sees any does not. It is that simple, and it is the mechanism underneath all of this.

What strength does is <strong>raise the bar of what the area tolerates before it complains</strong>. If your back is used to handling load regularly and under control, the trivial movement on an ordinary Tuesday falls well below that bar. If it is not, that same movement clears it.

That is why exercise is not just a treatment: it is the only thing that genuinely changes the odds of there being a next episode. Treatment resolves the pain you have now. Strength is what works on the one six months from now.

Where sport comes in

Staying active matters, but two things often get confused. Walking, swimming or simply moving every day is good for many reasons, and it helps. It is not, however, the same as strength training.

The adaptation we are talking about - tissue tolerating more - needs load. That means working with weight, your own body weight to begin with if necessary, and building up gradually. Training that covers legs, hips and trunk, because the back does not work alone: whatever the legs and hips do not do ends up being done by it.

And two things decide whether it works. <strong>Progress slowly</strong>, because injuries rarely come from the weight but from the sudden jump. And <strong>keep it going</strong>, because a programme done for eight weeks and abandoned leaves the back where it started within months. Something modest sustained for years beats a perfect plan that lasts one.

When to have it looked at

Starting to train on your own while your back is fine is reasonable. Where there is a recent episode, or pain that has been going on a while, an assessment is what determines where to start and what to leave for later.

And some signs are best not interpreted alone: pain travelling down the leg below the knee, pins and needles or loss of strength in the foot, or pain that has not eased at all over several weeks.

Frequently asked questions

If my back has gone, should I rest?

Less than people think. Staying active within what you can tolerate usually helps more than stopping altogether, even if the first few days call for a slower pace. What does not help is staying still and waiting for it to pass.

Can I do strength training if I already have back pain?

Generally yes, adapting what you do and how much load. What needs avoiding is the exercise that reproduces the pain as it is, not exercise itself. If the episode is recent, it is worth assessing first to know where to start.

Do I need a gym?

Not to begin with. You can progress a good way with your own body weight before needing equipment. What you do need is consistency and a gradual increase in demand: without that there is no adaptation.

How much do I have to train for it to work?

That depends on the person and their starting point, and it is part of what an assessment decides. What is clear is that what matters is not how hard the first few weeks are, but whether you are still doing it a year from now.
